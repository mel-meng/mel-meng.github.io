The starting problem

We have rainfall data and sewer-flow observations. We need a model simple enough to use in a drainage network, but flexible enough to reproduce the observed runoff volume.

Directly connected impervious area %

IF × PIMP

This is the percentage of the catchment that is directly connected impervious area in the New UK PR equation.

  • In this picture: connected roof + driveway draining to street/catch basin.
  • Rain on this part has little chance to infiltrate before reaching the drainage system.
  • This part reaches the drainage system directly, so the equation treats it as the connected impervious contribution.

Pervious + Non-effective Impervious %

100 − IF × PIMP

This is the remainder: true pervious area plus impervious area that is not effectively connected.

  • In this picture: lawn + garden + disconnected backyard patio.
  • The backyard patio is impervious, but it drains across grass first, so it is counted with the non-effective impervious part.
  • For this remainder, the tricky part is estimating where water is lost when the loss process is not obvious.

The equation terms

PR = IF · PIMP + (100 − IF · PIMP) · NAPI / PF
IF · PIMP Directly connected impervious area % Impervious area that is effectively connected to the drainage system.
100 − IF · PIMP Pervious + Non-effective Impervious % Everything not directly connected: pervious area plus non-effective impervious area. The model deliberately lumps these processes together.
NAPI / PF Wetness and capacity response A simple ratio: wetter weather increases runoff response; larger assumed capacity depth reduces runoff response.

The tricky loss part is not solved physically

For pervious and non-effective impervious areas, New UK PR does not explicitly simulate infiltration rates, soil suction, depression storage details, or disconnected surface routing.

Instead, it uses a simple assumption: these areas can withhold some water, and the amount of recent wet weather affects how much additional runoff they produce.

More recent wetness should mean more runoff from the pervious and non-effective impervious remainder.
More assumed capacity depth should mean less runoff for the same wetness.

Important: this is a teaching device, not a claim that PF is a measurable soil bucket.

The assumptions, stated clearly

Assumption 1 — directly connected impervious area is explicit. If pavement or roof area is effectively connected to the drainage system, it contributes runoff with relatively little delay or loss after initial losses.
Assumption 2 — the remaining area can be lumped. Pervious land and non-effective impervious land are treated together, even though their real processes differ.
Assumption 3 — the remainder responds to wetness. The wetter the weather has been, the more the remaining area behaves like runoff-producing area.
Assumption 4 — one simple ratio is used. The model uses NAPI/PF as a practical wetness-and-capacity response. This is a fitting structure, not a law of infiltration.
Assumption 5 — NAPI has weather memory. NAPI is an index derived from recent net rainfall, losses, drying, and decay. It has more physical connection than PF because it is driven by rainfall history.
Assumption 6 — PF is mainly a calibration scale. PF is the assumed capacity depth that controls how strongly a given wetness index turns into runoff from the lumped remainder.

IF is a connectivity knob

Use it to represent how much impervious land really behaves like directly connected impervious area.

higher IF more directly connected impervious area

lower IF more impervious area becomes non-effective impervious area

NAPI is the wetness memory

It comes from recent net rainfall and drying. Bigger NAPI means the catchment is wetter and more responsive.

higher NAPI more runoff from the pervious and non-effective impervious remainder

PF is the fitted scale

PF should be understood as an assumed capacity depth, not a directly measured soil bucket. It scales how much response a given NAPI produces.

higher PF less runoff for same wetness

How this differs from physical methods

New UK PR Empirical urban runoff-volume model. Splits catchment into directly connected impervious area plus pervious and non-effective impervious remainder.
Curve Number Event runoff method based around rainfall, abstraction, and potential retention. Useful but not the same as a dynamic urban wetness-response PR equation.
SWMM runoff More process-oriented surface hydrology: pervious/impervious subareas, infiltration/retention, and drainage-system applications.
The teaching distinction: New UK PR is mainly asking, “What percentage of rainfall volume should become sewer runoff?” SWMM-style runoff asks more directly, “How does water move through surface storage, infiltration, and drainage processes?”

Why UKWIR appears later

The weakness of New UK PR is also its teaching strength: it uses one simple wetness-and-capacity response for the pervious and non-effective impervious remainder. UKWIR was introduced to improve on that by separating paved and pervious response more explicitly.

What to calibrate against observations

The observations are rainfall in and sewer flow out. The model parameters are adjusted to mimic runoff volume and response behavior within defensible bounds.

IFIf the model gets the fast connected impervious contribution wrong across events, check connectivity assumptions, PIMP, and IF.
PFIf wet-weather volume from the pervious and non-effective impervious remainder is consistently too high or too low, PF is the main wetness-and-capacity scaling parameter.
Initial NAPIIf one event looks wrong because the starting condition was wetter/drier than assumed, check initial wetness.
Calibration warning: changing PF can hide errors in PIMP, IF, rainfall, flow monitoring, and initial losses. Treat PF as a powerful fitting knob, not proof of physical soil storage.

One-sentence explanation

New UK PR is a calibrated wetness-response model: directly connected impervious area is handled explicitly, while pervious and non-effective impervious area is passed through NAPI/PF to mimic observed volume behavior.
